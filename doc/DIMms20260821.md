# Abstract

Shallow cumulus clouds affect Earth's planetary albedo and explain the
spread in climate model sensitivity to atmospheric CO$_{2}$. Desiccating
the cloud layer by increased vertical mixing has been proposed to reduce
shallow cumulus clouds, but recent observations show that mass flux
explains mesoscale cloud fraction better than relative humidity. Here we
estimate the effect of mean surface temperature changes on the fraction
of shallow cumulus clouds, where, by hydrological balance, the
tropospheric circulation decreases --5 % per K mean surface temperature.
Local lower tropospheric moisture balance predicts shallow cumulus mass
flux and cloud fraction decrease by --5 % K^--1^ in proportion to the
reduced circulation. Increasing specific humidity +7% further reduces
the cloud fraction (--2.8%). Increasing precipitation at the expense of
moisture entrainment increases the clouds. In addition to these shallow
cumulus cloud fraction changes, subcloud fluctuations of RH change the
cloud fraction below 1 km by $\pm$`<!-- -->`{=html}8%, widening the
local estimated shallow cloud change to --4.7 to --7.8 % K^--1^. Shallow
cumulus clouds dim the planetary albedo with a shortwave radiative
feedback of +0.5 to +1.6 W m^--2^ and the net local cloud radiative
feedback of +0.4 to +1.3 W m^--2^ per K warming of mean surface
temperature.

# Introduction

Shallow cumulus clouds, with a mean cloud fraction of 0.1-0.4, occur
over \~15 % of Earth's oceans (Stevens et al. 2019, Jacob et al, 2020).
Satellite observations of the last decades show clearing of optically
thin low clouds represents the strongest contribution to shortwave cloud
feedback (Jin et al. 2026), and in models, shallow cumulus clearing
dominates changes in the occurrence of different cloud regimes (Zelinka
et al. 2023). Low cloud amounts, especially in subsiding regions, mostly
decrease with warming in climate models (ISCCP, Zelinka et al., 2023;
Webb and Lock, 2013; Webb et al. 2017, Jin et al. 2026). This positive
shallow cumulus feedback varies among models, widening the spread of
climate model projected temperature sensitivity to CO~2~ (Bony and
Dufresne, 2005; Bony et al., 2006).

In the mixing-induced low cloud mechanism, increased vertical mixing
reduces relative humidity and cloud fraction in a warmer climate
(Sherwood et al. 2014; Brient et al. 2016; Vial et al. 2017). CMIP3
models with more convective mixing were found to export more moisture
from the boundary layer under warming, and the strength of this
desiccation explains variations in their temperature sensitivity
(Sherwood et al. 2014). Mesoscale shallow cumulus cloud fraction is
correlated more strongly (*r* = 0.72) to cloud-base mass flux than to
relative humidity in EUREC4A field observations of Atlantic trade wind
cumulus clouds (Stevens et al. 2021; George et al. 2022), suggesting
shallow cumulus vertical mixing is positively correlated with clouds.

The mixing-induced low cloud mechanism scales with the amount of clouds
near cloud base. In the northern tropical Atlantic in Jan-Feb 2020,
airborne lidar and radar measured cloud-base cloud fraction of 5 % (Bony
et al., 2022; Vogel et al., 2022). Albedo due to clouds from the GOES
satellite show clouds below 1 km are responsible for 3-8% albedo,
depending on overlap (Fig. REFLALBEDO). CMIP6 models have fewer and
optically thicker clouds than observed over the northern tropical
Atlantic in January-February (Methods Fig. GOESISCCP c). Subcloud
turbulence moderates cloud base moisture and cloud fraction in
observations and LES (Nuijens et al., 2013; Bretherton et al., 2013;
Brient et al., 2016; Vial et al., 2017).

Most cloud tops are below 3 km, with a strong decrease of cloud tops
between 2-3 km (Fig. REFLALBEDO a,b), corresponding to the humidicline
in specific humidity associated with the trade inversion, observed by
radiosondes in Fig. REFLALBEDO c. This structure suggests convergence of
shallow cumulus moisture flux in the trade inversion, where subsidence
advection of dry air from above 3 km. The moisture and the cloud
profiles are key ingredients in a recipe for clouds and vertical
moisture flux, and how they change with global surface temperature.

## The DIM mechanism

The *decrease in mass flux* (DIM) mechanism predicts shallow cumulus
clouds decrease in proportion to the reduction of the mean tropical
large-scale circulation. In the DIM mechanism: (1) The saturation and
environmental humidity $q$ and cloud humidity $q_{c}$ increase +7% per K
mean surface temperature warming. (2) The large-scale tropical
circulation slows with by about --5% K^-1^ due to the specific humidity
and a +2% increase in precipitation (Held and Soden, 2006, HS06; Vecchi
and Soden, 2007). (3) Shallow cumulus regions have steady mean moisture
balance. Cumulus moisture fluxes balance the large-scale advection
drying $- \mathbf{U} \cdot \nabla q$,\
$$0 = - \partial/\partial z\left\lbrack M\left( q_{c} - q \right) - P \right\rbrack - \mathbf{U} \cdot \nabla q,
$$where $M\ $is the cumulus mass flux, $P$ is precipitation, $q_{c}$ is
the cloud humidity, $q$ is the environmental humidity, and
$- \mathbf{U} \cdot \nabla q$ is the mean large-scale advection.
Factoring out the change of the humidities, the mass flux is
proportional to the large-scale circulation, $M \propto \mathbf{U}$. (4)
The shallow cumulus cloud fraction $C\ $is proportional to mass flux,
$M = Cw$, where $w$ is the in-cloud vertical velocity. This is supported
by field observations where cloud base $M$ from mesoscale divergence was
correlated (*r*=0.72) to $C\ $with $\frac{C}{M} =$ 2.4-4.0 % (cm
s^--1^)^--1^ (Vogel et al., 2022). Assuming constant mean in-cloud
optical depth, changes in the satellite pixel- or scene-mean albedo due
to clouds $A$ are proportional to cloud fraction, and hence mass flux,
$A \propto C \propto M$.

The DIM hypothesis,
$\frac{\Delta C}{C} = \frac{\Delta M}{M} = \frac{\Delta W}{W},$ is a
simple mechanism projecting the change of low clouds in response to
surface temperature. This dimming generates a positive cloud radiative
feedback that scales with the present global mean albedo due to shallow
cumulus clouds.

## Cloud and flux model

Entraining and precipitating cloud plumes with different moisture sink
rates simulate cloud top height, humidity, mass- and moisture fluxes
(section Methods). Cloud total and liquid specific humidity are
illustrated for several moisture sink rates in Fig. REFLALBEDO. The
fraction of clouds at each moisture sink rate is set to the cloud top
height distribution below 4 km (Fig. REFLALBEDO). To predict the
response of shallow cumulus clouds to surface temperature, we separate
the projected thermodynamic and circulation changes from the
unpredictable small scale processes, such as turbulence and
microphysics, assumed not to change with mean temperature. Requiring
balance between large scale advective drying (methods Fig. LSADV) and
moisture convergence from clouds, we assess the effect of large-scale
circulation $W$ and environmental moisture $q$ on the cloud mass flux
$M$.

The moisture flux divergence balancing the mean moisture budget does not
constrain clouds below 1 km. These clouds are directly coupled to
subcloud moisture fluctuations (Albright, et al. 2023) produced and
dissipated by turbulence (Methods: LCL clouds).

# Results

## Climate experiments

A control simulation is run with the present moisture profile and large
scale drying. The fraction of clouds at each (entrainment +
precipitation) moisture sink rate is obtained by balancing the cloud-top
moisture flux divergence to the advective drying. Experiments predict
the mass flux for cloud updrafts with weaker large scale advective
drying and moister atmospheric soundings, corresponding to +1 K surface
temperature warming. Each experiment has a set of updrafts with
identical distribution of moisture sink rates as the control simulation.
The cloud top height and cloud flux is solved for each moisture sink
rate, specific humidity, and saturation specific humidity profile.

Table 1 shows changes
$\Delta C/C = \Delta M/M = \frac{M_{\exp}}{M_{ctl}} - 1$ for experiments
relative to the control experiment. Reducing only the large-scale drying
in experiment adv-5% decreases mass flux by $\Delta M/M_{ctl} =$ --5 %.
Increasing cloud base vapor flux $E_{cb}$ (not shown) has no effect on
the mass flux. In addition to the reduced advective drying, in DIM,
specific humidity and saturation specific humidity increase +7% at
constant RH, further reducing clouds to 7.7 %. Increasing specific
humidity increases the vertical moisture gradient and the cumulus
cloud-environment humidity difference $q_{c} - q$. The mass flux $M$
required for the cloud moisture flux $M(q_{c} - q)$ decreases --2.7%,
about 1.5 times more than just decreasing the large-scale advection
(adv-5%).

Though specific humidity decreases clouds, cloud base RH increases them.
Starting from DIM, also increasing cloud base RH by
$RH = RH_{DIM} + 0.05\left( 1 - RH_{DIM} \right)(2z_{CB} - z)/2z_{CB}$
in lclRH+0.003 results in +2.7% more clouds than the DIM experiment
(--5.03% less clouds than the control simulation). Surface RH and wind
speed both increase to damp the surface evaporation change to +2%
(Richter and Xie 2008, Lorenz et al. 2010). The effect of subcloud
humidity changes on cloud-base clouds is explored below.

Changes in mesoscale cloud organization affect cloud fraction. Though
the model does not account for mesoscale vertical velocity anomalies,
increasing the precipitation efficiency $x$ by +1.6% in experiment
x=0.64 emulates the effect larger and more clustered clouds that
precipitate more and entrain less than the control simulation
($x = 0.63$). The total sink rate stays the same, but clouds generate
more precipitation in x=0.64, and cloud fraction increases +0.88%
relative to the control. Precipitation opposes the cloud updraft
moisture flux, so the experiment with more precipitation requires more
clouds for the same total eddy moisture flux. Increasing the
precipitation by increasing precipitation efficiency +1.6% slightly
mitigates the reduction of cloud fraction in the DIM experiment to a
total reduction of only --6.88%, relative to the control.

## Mass flux and cloud albedo

The cloud model generates cloud water and vertical velocity profiles for
thousands of \~280x280 km^2^ GOES satellite reflectance scenes within
2020 Jan-Feb 12.5-16 °N, 60-49° W. The scene-mean mass flux binned as a
function of reflectance and scene-mean albedo due to clouds (calculated
as cloud albedo-weighted cloud fraction $A$) is shown in Fig.
REFLMASSFL. The near proportionality of mass flux to cloud albedo
suggests that clouds have a mean albedo-weighted in-cloud updraft
velocity $w_{A} \approx$ 67 cm s^-1^. This proportionality of cloud
albedo to mass flux $A \propto M$ is used to infer the proportional
relationship between cloud albedo and mass flux. The slope
$dA/dM = \ $`<!-- -->`{=html}1.5 % \[cm s^--1^\]^-1^ is slightly lower
than $dC/dM$ observed by lidar and radar cloud fraction and mass flux
from dropsonde circles in EUREC4A (2.4 - 4.0 % \[cm s^--1^\]^-1^; Vogel
et al. 2022). This is likely because the lidar-radar cloud fraction
$C\ $detects optically thinner clouds that are underestimated by the
GOES albedo-weighted cloud fraction $A$. Only about half (*r*^2^ = 0.52)
of the mass flux variance observed in the field is correlated with the
clouds, additionally increasing the observed $dC/dM$ estimate compared
to the modeled mass flux.

The modeled mass flux increases slightly for scenes with brighter albedo
$A$ due to clouds, implying in-cloud updraft velocity
$w_{A} = dM/dA = 0.54 + 1.2A$, increases with $A$. Greater mesoscale
clustering of nearer, larger, and deeper clouds for higher scene-mean
albedo might explain the increasing in-cloud vertical velocity. The mass
flux model and JOANNE observations describe shallow cumulus clouds
modulated by mass flux $M$. Stratocumulus clouds, coupled to subcloud
turbulence, are not expected to be correlated to the mass flux.

## Cloud-base cloud fraction

Climate feedbacks depend on changes in clouds found in a transition
layer near the shallow cumulus cloud base, where they are directly
coupled to the subcloud turbulence and moisture (Albright, et al. 2023).
These cloud-base clouds are modeled from the distribution of subcloud
relative humidity fluctuations produced and dissipated by turbulence
(Methods: LCL clouds). Cloud-base cloud fraction changes
$\Delta C_{CB}/C_{CB} =$ ±8% K^--1^ from sub-cloud turbulent
fluctuations of surface relative humidity (RH) and turbulent moisture
fluxes. The changes in cloud base clouds are used to estimate
uncertainty of the total shallow cloud feedback.

Surface evaporation is assumed to increase +2% K^--1^ (Richter and Xie,
2008; Lorenz et al. 2010), in proportion to precipitation, due to a
combination of increasing relative humidity and decreasing wind speed
with temperature. The cloud-base cloud fraction changes proportional to
a power of $d\ln{{(q}_{s}/E)}/dT = 5\%$ K^--1^. The power depends on
change of the surface relative humidity, and ratio of the cloud base
saturation deficit to the surface saturation deficit. Clouds decrease
--8% when the cloud-base saturation deficit is invariant and clouds
increase +8% when it scales linearly with surface saturation deficit.

About 19% of satellite-observed cloud albedo is due to clouds below 1 km
(Fig. REFLALBEDO b). Assuming these are cloud-base clouds that change
relatively by ±8% K^--1^, and 81% are shallow cumulus that decrease
--7.7 % K^--1^, the total cloud fraction below 4 km decreases by --4.7
to --7.8 % K^--1^. This corresponds to local low cloud shortwave dimming
of +1.0 to +1.6 W m^--2^ K^--1^ and total cloud radiative feedback of
+0.8 to +1.3 W m^--2^ K^--1^.

# Summary

The mean moisture budget in the descending branch of the Hadley
circulation provides a recipe for shallow cumulus clouds. An ensemble of
cumulus clouds in hydrological balance with the large-scale circulation
drying reproduces the ratio of mass flux to cloud albedo observed in
EUREC4A (Vogel et al. 2022), and extends it to climatological mean mass
flux and cloud fraction. Experiments demonstrate the decrease in mass
flux (DIM) mechanism, for changes expected from a balanced water budget,
with the weakening hydrological overturning circulation expected from
global surface warming (HS06).

Weather and climate model cloud parameterizations satisfy many
constraints, few of which make them suited to faithfully simulate the
cloud fraction of shallow cumulus clouds (Vial et al., 2017, Nuijens et
al., 2015). Our shallow cumulus model represents cloud-scale processes
with a spectrum of total moisture sink rates representing lateral
entrainment and precipitation. Each moisture sink rate determines a
profile of cloud humidity $q_{c}$ and a cloud top height.

The DIM mechanism projects shallow cumulus clouds decrease --7.7 %,
mostly from decreasing large-scale advection, and secondarily from
increasing specific humidity. Different proportionalities of the
relative of changes of clouds, $\Delta C/C$, mass flux $\Delta M/M$, and
large-scale advection drying over the shallow cumulus region would alter
this projection. The shallow cumulus cloud fraction is assumed
proportional to the mass flux $\Delta C/C = \Delta M/M$. The definition
of the mass flux $M = wC_{up}\ $implies the average in-cloud updraft
velocity $w$ and the ratio $\frac{C}{C_{up}}$, the area of the cloud to
the area of the updraft responsible for the mass flux, are constant
(Siebesma et al., 2003; Vogel et al., 2020). The moisture budget balance
assumes
$(\Delta M/M)\lbrack\Delta(q_{c} - q)/(q_{c} - q)\rbrack = (\Delta\mathbf{U}/\mathbf{U})\lbrack\Delta(\nabla q)/(\nabla q)\rbrack$.
Disproportional changes of the large-scale moisture gradient
$\Delta q\ $to the cloud-environment moisture difference $q_{c} - q$
would change the proportionality of mass flux $M$ to the large-scale
mean circulation $\mathbf{U}$.

The DIM mechanism predicts the change in area of low clouds responsible
for the moisture flux but does not account for secondary desiccation of
optically thick stratocumulus clouds found near cloud base in climate
models. Radiative effects are similar for stratocumulus and shallow
cumulus clouds (Tselioudis et al., 2021; Zelinka et al. 2025).
Increasing clouds coupled to the subcloud humidity could compensate the
radiative effect of losing shallow cumulus clouds. Mixing-induced low
cloud feedback, wherein mixing by precipitating convection dries lower
levels and evaporates extensive and optically thick stratocumulus
clouds, describes a mechanism for this compensation. Though subsidence
is a common precondition for stratocumulus clouds, all else being equal,
greater subsidence reduces stratocumulus clouds (Myers and Norris,
2014).

Convective plumes balancing large-scale drying do not accurately
parameterize the 19% of clouds with cloud tops below 1 km over the
tropical Atlantic study region. Rather, these cloud base clouds are
coupled to subcloud humidity fluctuations. The fraction of these
humidity fluctuations resulting in clouds may increase or decrease,
depending on the degree to which surface and cloud-base RH increases,
broadening our estimate of low cloud fraction change to $\Delta C/C =$
--7.8 to --4.7 % K^--1^.

Mesoscale variations of clouds have been observed to be related to
mesoscale divergence. Climate models that reproduce the mesoscale
coupling between clouds and mass flux have weaker climate sensitivity,
suggesting that mesoscale organization mitigates positive low cloud
feedback. The present study is not suited to assess the effect of
mesoscale deviations from the steady balance of the moisture budget, but
some of the effects of wider and more clustered clouds are simulated by
decreasing moisture entrainment and increasing precipitation. In this
case, cloud fraction increases to offset the drying effect of
precipitation.

# Methods

## Low clouds in trade cumulus regions {#low-clouds-in-trade-cumulus-regions .Heading3}

Trade cumulus clouds are intermittent shallow convection in widespread
regions of mean subsidence balancing deeper convection elsewhere. Figure
GOESISCCP shows cloud top height and cloud optical thickness histograms
observed from the 2-km pixel GOES observations in the Northern Atlantic
shallow cumulus region 12.5-16 °N, 60-49 °W, 2020 Jan 15 - February 20
from a GOES retrieval (Minnis et al., 2008; W. Smith, NASA SATCORP) and
the CERES Flux By Cloud Type (FBCT; Sun et al., 2022). Most of the GOES
clouds are below 680 hPa (37% cloud fraction) with cloud optical
thickness $\tau =$ 0.3-9.4 (Fig. GOESISCCP a). The FBCT histogram is
similar (36% cloud fraction below 680 hPa; Fig. GOESISCCP b) but has
(2-3x) greater optical thickness than GOES, possibly because of the
greater independence of cloud fraction and optical thickness for the
1-km MODIS pixels used by FBCT. FBCT also observes more 800-680 hPa
cloud tops (15%) than GOES (7.4%).

Low clouds in five CMIP6 preindustrial control simulations in the Cloud
Feedback Model Intercomparison Project (CFMIP) have greater optical
thickness than observed over the northern tropical Atlantic in
January-February. Model-mean cloud optical thickness is mainly $\tau =$
1.3 to 23, with few clouds thinner than $\tau$ = 3.6, (Fig. GOESISCCP
c).

There are two modes in the vertical structure of the shallow cumulus
clouds (Fig. REFLALBEDO; Jacob et al. 2020). The first, just above cloud
base at 0.5-1.0 km, has high frequency of occurrence but low albedo and
reflectance. Many of these lowest clouds are generated in boundary layer
updrafts smaller than the \~700 depth of the subcloud layer and thus do
not fill the 2-km GOES pixels, lowering the effective cloud albedo.
Clouds not filling the pixel and allowing it to be partly transparent to
the longwave, may account for the vertical spread of the cloud top
height of the lower mode, estimated from cloud top infrared emission.
The second vertical mode is observed below the trade inversion at
1.5-2.5 km. It has a cloud fraction half as large as the cloud base
mode, but clouds above 1.5 km are responsible for an albedo of 0.05 of
the total 0.07 integrated over the lower 4 km.

Top-of-atmosphere cloud radiative effect (CRE) of the low clouds (p\>680
hPa) is computed from the Zelinka et al. (2012) cloud radiative kernel
using the cloud top pressure and optical thickness from GOES (Fig.
GOESISCCP c), and FBCT radiative fluxes in each bin minus clear-sky
radiative flux (Fig. GOESISCCP d). CRE changes for climate experiments
are estimated from cloud amount changes predicted by our shallow cumulus
cloud model.

Mean cloud reflectance and albedo due to clouds are computed at each
10-m height from 0.5-4 km from visible optical depth of daylight 2x2
km^2^ pixels (Fig. REFLALBEDO). Pixels at solar zenith angle \>75 ° and
deeper than 4 km are excluded. Albedo due to clouds is calculated for
each cloudy pixel with a two-stream approximation for inelastic
scattering, $\alpha_{c} = (1 - g)\tau/(2 + (1 - g)\tau)$, from the cloud
optical thickness $\tau$ and asymmetry parameter $g \approx 0.85$
(Coakley and Chylek 1975, Tornow et al. 2020). The albedo-weighted cloud
fraction (Fig. REFLALBEDO) below 4 km is a proxy for the scene-mean
albedo due to clouds $A$ of Northern Tropical Atlantic maritime clouds
during EUREC4A (Jan 15-Feb 12 2020 at 12.5-16 °N, 60-49 °W). GOES mean
$A$ is slightly less than mean reflectance. The GOES cloud fraction
(Figs. REFLALBEDO and GOESISCCP) from counting cloudy pixels is greater
than $A$ because $A$ is less than unity for cloudy pixels with finite
optical thickness.

## The large-scale moisture budget

Clouds with average albedo $A_{i}$ have cloud tops that reach $h_{i}$ in
GOES satellite images (Fig. REFLALBEDO). We discretize the all-sky mean
eddy flux $G = aF$, where $F$ is the in-cloud updraft flux, to clouds
reaching height $h_{i}$. We use cloud albedo-weighted cloud fraction
$a_{i}$. This fraction is proportional to the radiative effect of the
clouds and approaches cloud fraction for clouds with high optical
thickness. The all-sky mean eddy flux of moisture $G$ is the integral of
the large-scale source $S_{LS}$,\
$$G(z) = G_{CB} + \int_{z_{CB}}^{z}{S_{LS}\ dz^{'}},
$$with the cloud base moisture flux $G_{CB}$ as the boundary condition.
The large-scale moisture source $S_{LS}$ is drying due to subsidence and
horizontal advection.![](media/image4.png){width="4.886076115485564in"

We partition the moisture flux into a vertically uniform flux for each
cloud top height $h_{i}$. The discrete flux difference
$G_{i} = G\left( h_{i + 1} \right) - G\left( h_{i} \right)$ is assumed
to be carried by the clouds with (albedo-weighted) cloud fraction
$a_{i}$. The cloud top height $h_{i}$ of each modeled shallow cumulus
cloud is determined by its moisture sink rate parameter.

The all-sky flux $G = aF$ is the product of cloud area and in-cloud flux
$F$. The all-sky mass flux $M = aw$ likewise corresponds to the in-cloud
vertical velocity $w$. The model divides clouds and their fluxes into
categories $i:$\
$${M_{i} = a_{i}w_{i},
}{G_{i} = a_{i}F_{i} = a_{i}w_{i}(\Delta q)_{i} = M_{i}(\Delta q)_{i},
}{F_{i} = w_{i}(\Delta q)_{i}
}$$where total fluxes are summed over the categories,
$G = \sum_{i}^{}G_{i}$, $F = \sum_{i}^{}F_{i}$, and
$M = \sum_{i}^{}M_{i}$, $w = \sum_{i}^{}{a_{i}w_{i}/A}$. Figure LSADV b
shows the distribution of in-sky fluxes $F_{i} = G_{i}/a_{i}$ in each
cloud top height category.

Water fluxes by clouds are diagnosed to balance a simple vertical water
budget for the trade cumulus cloud layer. The updraft area fraction is
calculated over large (1000 km)^2^ regions, assuming updraft cores are
cloudy with saturated specific humidity. Cloud mass flux is initialized
at cloud base to match the mean observed surface evaporation minus
precipitation. From the moisture closure, the in-cloud eddy flux due to
cumulus updrafts and precipitation is
$F_{i}(z) = F_{c,i}(z) + F_{p,i}(z)$. Cloud updrafts are responsible for
most of the eddy flux. A small part is contributed by precipitation. We
will first model the water in the clouds, then their conversion to
precipitation.

### Cumulus cloud model

A budget for steady total water $q_{c}$ in the updraft with velocity $w$
depends on its entrainment and precipitation sinks. The equations for
the suspended liquid $q_{l}$ and water vapor $q_{v}$ specific humidity
in the cloud are\
$${\frac{\partial q_{l}}{\partial t} = - w\left( \frac{\partial q_{l}}{\partial z} \right) - w\gamma - \epsilon wq_{l} - \alpha wq_{l}
}{\frac{\partial q_{v}}{\partial t} = - w\left( \frac{\partial q_{v}}{\partial z} \right) + w\gamma - \epsilon w\left( q_{v} - q \right),
}$$where $\epsilon$ (km^--1^) is the lateral entrainment coefficient,
$\alpha$ is the autoconversion of cloud liquid to precipitation, and
$\gamma = \left( \frac{dq_{s}}{dz} \right)_{m} = \left( \frac{dq_{s}}{dT} \right)\left( \frac{dT}{dz} \right)_{m} < 0$
is the moist adiabatic condensation rate of water vapor per unit height.
The dependence on $\gamma$ is eliminated in the total cloud water
$q_{c}$ equation,\
$$\frac{\partial q_{c}}{\partial t} = 0 = - w(\frac{\partial q_{c}}{\partial z}) - \epsilon w(q_{c} - q) - \alpha wq_{l}
$$QTBALANCE.$
$The updraft advects moisture upwards, but the solution for $q_{T}$ is
quantitatively insensitive to the updraft velocity and to the moisture
flux. In the cloud, $q_{l} = q_{c} - q_{s}$, autoconversion removes
cloud liquid, relaxing total water toward $q_{s}$, and entrainment
relaxes total water toward $q$. Thus, equation QTBALANCE is written in
terms of the total vertical sink rate $\epsilon + \alpha$,\
$$\frac{\partial q_{c}}{\partial z} = - (\epsilon + \alpha)\left( q_{c} - \widetilde{q} \right),
$$QRELAX$
$relaxing $q_{c}$ to the intermediate humidity
$\widetilde{q} = \frac{(\epsilon q + \alpha q_{s})}{(\epsilon + \alpha)} = (1 - x)q + xq_{s}$.
The total sink rate $\epsilon + \alpha$ determines the cloud water,
water flux, and cloud top height. We call the ratio
$x = \frac{\alpha}{(\epsilon + \alpha)}$ of the autoconversion to the
total moisture sink the *precipitation efficiency*. Cloud liquid and
water vapor profiles are computed for many total sink rates
$(\epsilon + \alpha)_{i}$, resulting in the cloud top heights $h_{i}$
(Fig. REFLALBEDO).

The profiles of $q_{c}$ predicted by this entraining-precipitating
updraft model agree with the most humid air sampled on occasion by
radiosondes. Cloud height (where $q_{c}$ reaches 0) is limited by
entrainment of relatively dry air between 1.5-3 km, representing the
mean distribution of the trade inversion. The clouds occupy a range of
depths representative of trade cumulus clouds, with deeper clouds for
lower rates of entrainment and autoconversion. The intermediate total
sink rate of $\epsilon + \alpha \approx 0.5\ {km}^{- 1}$ gives maximum
cloud liquid specific humidity exceeding 0.8 g kg^--1^ and cloud top
height of \~3 km.

## Precipitation and updraft moisture fluxes {#precipitation-and-updraft-moisture-fluxes .Heading3}

Shallow cumulus clouds generate weak precipitation, reversing a fraction
of the updraft total cloud water flux. Precipitation water flux within a
cloud $F_{P} < 0$ is generated by autoconversion $\alpha$ from cloud
liquid. For the updraft with no storage of cloud liquid water, the
precipitation source is\
$$- \frac{\partial F_{p}}{\partial z} = - \alpha wq_{l} = xw\left( \frac{\partial q_{l}}{\partial z} + \gamma \right),
$$where $\partial q_{l}/\partial z + \gamma$ is the total moisture
source in the updraft.

The solution for each cloud type $i$ is found by numerically integrating
the precipitation downward from $F = 0$ at cloud top. The precipitation
sink, $\frac{\partial F_{p,i}}{\partial z} = \alpha_{i}w_{i}q_{l,i},$
instantaneously removes liquid water from the cloud and the column.

An example of the all-sky fluxes is computed for a single cloud with,
$\epsilon + \alpha = 1.5\ {km}^{- 1}$, $h = 1.9$ km and all-sky
cloud-base flux $G\left( z_{CB} \right) =$ 145 W m^--2^
$= 6.0 \times 10^{- 5}$ kg m^--2^ s^--1^ (Fig. QMASSFLX). For this case,
$F$ goes to zero at 8 km. Precipitation increases downward below the
cloud top. At a precipitation efficiency of \~0.5, the cloud-base
precipitation $P_{CB} = \left| F_{P} \right|$ equals the mean surface
precipitation, $P_{0} = \ $`<!-- -->`{=html}25 W m^--2^
$= 9.0 \times 10^{- 6}\ $kg m^--2^ s^--1^, observed on the research
vessel *Ron Brown* during EUREC4A/ATOMIC. Evaporation was $E_{0} =$ 180
W m^--2^ $= 6.5 \times 10^{- 5}$ kg m^--2^ s^--1^. The mass flux is
$M = \frac{G_{cld}}{q_{T} - q}$ (Fig. QMASSFLX). Most of the flux (\~90
%) is below 3 km in the trade cumulus layer. Most of the updraft
moisture $q_{T}$ is vapor, and the moist updraft $q_{T} - q > 0$
generates upward moisture flux, even above the cloud.

The cloud (a) total specific humidity and (b) cloud-environment humidity
difference as a function of height and total sink rate parameter is
shown for an ensemble of sink rates in Fig. WPROF. This is used to
compute (c) cloud-mean vertical velocity for each cloud. The total mass
flux is $M = \sum a_{i}w_{i}$, where the total cloud area is
$a = \sum a_{i}$.

### Numerical integration of cloud fluxes

At each height and for each cloud category $i$,
$w_{i}q_{l,i} = F_{c,i}\frac{q_{l,i}}{\left( q_{c,i} - q \right)}$ and
$F_{c,i} = F - F_{P_{i}}$. The precipitation equation is written in
differential form,\
$$dF_{P,i} = \frac{\alpha_{i}q_{l,i}}{\left( q_{T,i} - q \right)\left( F - F_{P,i} \right)dz}.
$$Precipitation is integrated downward, from
$F_{P,i}\left( h_{i} \right) = 0$ at the top $h_{i}\ $of each cloud, in
trapezoidal vertical steps with decreasing superscript $k$,\
$$F_{P,i}^{k} = - \frac{- \left( 1 + c_{i}^{k} \right)F_{P,i}^{k + 1} + c^{k}F^{k} + c^{k + 1}F^{k + 1}}{1 - c^{k}},
$$where
$c_{i}^{k} = \frac{\left\lbrack \alpha_{i}q_{l,i}^{k}\ dz \right\rbrack}{\left\lbrack 2\left( q_{T,i}^{k} - q^{k} \right) \right\rbrack}$.

### Cloud radiative effect

The observed GOES low ($p_{c} >$ 680 hPa) cloud fraction
$C_{0}\left( \tau,p_{c} \right)\ $counts pixels in cloud optical
thickness $\tau$ and cloud top pressure $p_{c}$ bins (Minnis et al.
2008). The shortwave (SW), longwave (LW), and total cloud radiative
effect (CRE) is estimated from the Zelinka et al. (2012) cloud radiative
kernel $K(\tau,p_{c})$:
${CRE}_{0} = \sum_{\tau,p_{c}}^{}{C_{0}\left( \tau,p_{c} \right)\ K(\tau,p_{c})}$.
Total, shortwave (SW), and longwave (LW) CRE changes
$\Delta CRE = {CRE}_{0}\left( \frac{\Delta C}{C_{ctl}} \right)$ are
multiplied by the relative modeled change in the shallow cumulus albedo
$\frac{\Delta C}{C_{ctl}} = \Delta M/M_{ctl}$ for the shallow cumulus
experiments (Table 1). The CRE for all low clouds is computed by
weighting the relative changes of shallow cumulus (SC, $f_{SC} = 0.81$)
and CB clouds,\
$$\Delta CRE = {CRE}_{0}\left( f_{SC}\frac{\Delta C_{SC}}{C_{SC}} + \left( 1 - f_{SC} \right)\frac{\Delta C_{CB}}{C_{CB}} \right).$$

## Cloud-base clouds

Large-eddy simulation (LES; (Bretherton et al. 2013, Blossey et
al. 2013) and observations (Bony et al., 2021; Vogel et al., 2022; Fig.
REFLALBEDO) have peak cloud fraction of about 5 % near cloud base. We
estimate change in this cloud-base cloud fraction from mean and
variations of the subcloud relative humidity (RH), modeled from moisture
gradients and fluxes. We model clouds for constant RH (Held and Shell
XXXX), and for RH increasing to the damp the surface evaporation to +2 %
K^--1^ (Richter and Xie 2008; Lorenz et al. 2010).

The cloud-base cloud fraction is the fraction of air from the moist
subcloud boundary layer thermals that reaches saturation at cloud base,
$C_{CB} = \left( 1 - {erf}(s) \right)/2$ for a normal distribution of
subcloud RH, where
$s = \frac{\left( 1 - {\overline{RH}}_{CB} \right)}{\sigma_{RH}}$ is the
mean relative saturation deficit normalized by the standard deviation of
RH (Sommeria and Deardorff, 1977; Mellor 1977). The cloud-base cloud
fraction observed by combined radar and lidar is 0.054
$\pm$`<!-- -->`{=html}0.031% in EUREC4A (BASTALIAS, Bony et al., 2022;
Vogel et al., 2022). Cloud base cloud fraction $C_{CB} = 0.055\ $matches
the observations for $s_{0} = 1.13$. Cloud base cloud fraction changes
as $C_{CB} \propto s^{- 3.2}$.

Production-dissipation balance for the subcloud specific humidity
variance $\sigma_{q}^{2}$ gives\
$$0 = - u^{*}q^{*}\left( \frac{\partial\overline{q}}{\partial z} \right) - \chi_{q}.
$$Specific humidity variance is produced by flux
$u^{*}q^{*} = \overline{w^{'}q^{'}}$ and gradient
$\frac{\partial\overline{q}}{\partial z}$, and dissipates with rate
$\chi_{q} = \frac{k_{1}u^{*}\sigma_{q}^{2}}{z_{CB}}$ from turbulent
stirring and viscous mixing of humidity fluctuations, where $k_{1}$ is a
constant and $z_{CB}$ is cloud base height. Shear production is greater
than buoyancy production, which is neglected. The friction velocity
$u^{*} = {\overline{u^{'}w^{'}}}^{1/2}$ cancels and the humidity
variance is
$\sigma_{q}^{2} = \frac{q^{*}\left( q_{sfc} - q_{CB} \right)}{k_{1}}.\ $Then
the RH standard deviation at cloud base, $
$$$\sigma_{RH} = \frac{\sigma_{q}}{q_{s,CB}} = \left\lbrack \frac{q^{*}\left( q_{sfc} - q_{CB} \right)}{\left( k_{1}q_{s,CB}^{2} \right)} \right\rbrack^{\frac{1}{2}},
$$depends on the humidity scale $q^{*}$, the subcloud mean humidity
difference $q_{sfc} - q_{CB}$, and the cloud-base saturation specific
humidity $q_{s,CB}$. We assume the specific humidity gradient scales
with the saturation specific humidity as +7% K^--1^, so the change of
$\frac{\left( q_{sfc} - q_{CB} \right)}{q_{s,CB}}$ cancels and\
$$d\ln\sigma_{RH} = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)\left\lbrack d\ln q^{*} - \ d\ln q_{s,CB} \right\rbrack.
$$The surface evaporation $E = \rho Lu^{*}q^{*}$ and cloud-base moisture
flux increase +2 % K^--1^ to balance the increase of precipitation. From
factoring the evaporation,*\
*$$d\ln E = d\ln U + d\ln q_{s} + d\ln\left( 1 - {\overline{RH}}_{sfc} \right) = d\ln u^{*} + d\ln q^{*}.$$

The change of the mean normalized saturation deficit is\
$$d\ln s = d\ln\left( 1 - {\overline{RH}}_{CB} \right) + \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)\left( d\ln q_{s,CB} - d\ln q^{*} \right),
$$with $d\ln q^{*} = d\ln E - d\ln U$. We write $d\ln s$ as the
function\
$$d\ln s = - c(b - 1/2)(d\ln q_{s} - d\ln E)
$$of known $d\ln{{(q}_{s}/E)}/dT = + 5\ \%\ K^{- 1}$ and parameters $b$
and $c$, each bounded by 0 and 1.

The RH damping,
$c = \frac{d\ln{(1 - {\overline{RH}}_{sfc})}}{\left( d\ln E - d\ln q_{s} \right)} > 0$,
is the reduction of the increase of surface evaporation by the increase
of surface RH. For constant surface wind speed, $c = 1$, and increasing
surface RH alone limits the flux to $d\ln E = + 2\%$. For $c = 0$,
surface RH is constant and reduced wind speed entirely limits the flux.
Cloud base cloud fraction changes only if surface RH changes; clouds do
not change for $c = 0$.

The sign of $\Delta C_{CB}$ depends on the power,
$b = \frac{d\ln\left( 1 - {\overline{RH}}_{CB} \right)}{d\ln{(1 - {\overline{RH}}_{sfc})}}$,
with which the cloud base saturation deficit scales with the surface
saturation deficit: $b = 0$ for constant cloud base RH, and $b = 1$ for
cloud base mean saturation deficit proportional to surface saturation
deficit $1 - {\overline{RH}}_{CB} \propto 1 - {\overline{RH}}_{sfc}$.
Relative changes $\Delta s/s$ and $\Delta C_{CB}/C_{CB}$ are monotonic
with the sensitivity $b$ to cloud base saturation deficit and symmetric
about $b = 1/2$. At $b =$ 1/2, increasing mean RH and narrowing its
standard deviation $\sigma_{RH}$ perfectly balance, and there is no
change in cloud base cloud fraction.

The power
$- c\left( b - \frac{1}{2} \right) \in \lbrack - 0.5,\ 0.5\rbrack$
multiplies +5 % K^--1^ to give $d\ln C_{CB} = - 3.2d\ln s$ (Fig.
BCSENS). Clouds increase at most by $\Delta C_{CB}/C_{CB}$ = +0.08 for
$\frac{\Delta s}{s} = - 0.025$, and decrease by at most
$\Delta C_{CB}/C_{CB}$ = --0.08 for $\frac{\Delta s}{s} = + 0.025$.

of cloud-base clouds depends on subtle changes in surface humidity that
may be compensated by the effect of wind on the surface evaporation; and
to what degree cloud base humidity increases with surface humidity.
